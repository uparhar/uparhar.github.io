Ogopogo, the self-driving sub

In progressSept 2025 to now

Ogopogo is our team's robot for RoboSub, and nobody drives it. It finds its own way around an underwater course, spots targets with a camera, and fires torpedoes without anyone touching a controller. I'm the electrical subteam lead, so power, the main controller and the firmware on every micro in the hull are my problem. I also mentor five teammates on PCB design and debugging.

The main controller board

  • Custom STM32F765 board, designed from scratch in Altium and hand-soldered by me.
  • PWM out to the thrusters, onboard IMU and magnetometer, the kill switch, plus CAN, I²C, SPI and UART to everything else.
  • Swapped in cheaper regulators, passives and protection parts. Projected board cost dropped 55%.
  • Built an FTDI JTAG/SWD adapter to go with it, which cut programming setup time by over 60%.
  • First power-up, the debugger couldn't see the chip. Turned out to be a broken SWDIO trace, so I clipped the probe straight onto the pin and kept going.

Hearing a pinger underwater // in progress

  • A pinger marks the target. Four hydrophones each hear it at a slightly different time, and that's enough to work out which way it is.
  • My analog front-end board amplifies the signal and bandpass-filters out thruster and ambient noise.
  • Verilog on a Zynq FPGA measures the time difference of arrival across the array and turns it into a bearing.
  • The bearing goes to the Jetson over Ethernet, and the sub steers toward it.

Wiring up the whole sub // 2026–27 design

  • A Jetson Orin Nano handles vision and sends motor commands over UART to the STM32 main board.
  • CAN links the main board to an actuator board (arm, dropper, torpedoes) and two battery management boards, one per battery hull.
  • The power distribution unit is a stacking backplane: 16 V in from the battery hulls, 12 V out. Boards can be pulled and revised without rewiring the sub.
  • STM32 firmware runs on FreeRTOS. The hull watches itself for leaks and tracks pressure, temperature and humidity.
  • I've pitched in on vision too: a YOLO11 detector trained on images auto-labelled with Grounding DINO.

Catching algae blooms on a budget

In progressDec 2025 to now

This one started as a first-year class project. I built a buoy to keep an eye on Okanagan Lake, some City of Kelowna councillors and the Okanagan Basin Water Board got interested, and I pitched it to Dr. Peleato's lab at UBCO. Now I'm working with faculty and a PhD researcher on a cheap sensor that spots cyanobacteria, the stuff behind toxic algal blooms.

PHOTOsensor prototype on the bench

The fluorescence sensor

  • It's an in-situ fluorometer: an LED excites the cyanobacteria pigments, and an optically filtered photodetector measures the light they emit back.
  • I'm designing the analog detection and filtering circuit. It amplifies the photodetector current and rejects ambient light, with the goal of resolving signals below WHO alert levels.
  • Spec'd a high-PSRR power stage so supply ripple stays out of the measurement.
  • Wrote the interface docs between the sensor and its host platform so the lab and I can build in parallel. They got reused in a research funding application.
  • Cost target: about 30% of a commercial probe.
